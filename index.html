Long-context generation is a sampling problem (when you turn up the heat)

Why do open-weight models get worse the longer they generate? This 29-model study (1B to 122B, across SWE-bench agents, long CoT, long-form writing, and needle retrieval to 1M tokens) says it is often the sampler, not the weights: samplers that keep their candidate pool small and steady as temperature rises don't lose accuracy. That reframes a common debugging reflex — before reaching for more training or a bigger checkpoint, try constraining the decoding rule. Directly actionable for anyone running open models in agent loops where generation gets long.

A dead star spinning every 8 minutes pulses radio waves beside a Sun-like companion

A white dwarf 5,000 light-years away rotates once every eight minutes and fires long-period radio pulses while orbiting a G-type, Sun-like star — the first time these strange radio emitters have been seen in a system with a solar twin rather than a red dwarf. Over 100 hours of radio data let the team track the white dwarf around its orbit and pin the pulses to the dead star itself, a first for this still-new class with only about a dozen known members. Bonus: they watched it pass behind a stellar prominence, detecting magnetic storms on a star across the galaxy. Published in Nature Astronomy (DOI 10.1038/s41550-026-03016-z).

Sci Lund University / Science Advances Science ›Discuss › Must-Read

An ancient virus switched off a brain-development gene in chimps that stays on in humans

Lund University researchers found that PTERV1, an endogenous retrovirus that infected chimpanzee ancestors millions of years ago, silences the gene LINC00662 in chimps — while the same gene runs hot in early human brain development. The causal chain is beautifully closed: CRISPR excision of the viral insertion in chimp cells restores the gene, and silencing it in human brain organoids impairs neurite extension and nerve-cell maturation genes. A virus that never entered the human genome may be one reason our brains developed differently. Published in Science Advances.

Sci Frontiers / University of Arkansas Science ›Discuss › Must-Read

Dinosaurs were probably eating seaweed 100 million years ago

Carbon isotope ratios in tooth enamel from fossils along the Western Interior Seaway and ancient Gulf coast show coastal Cretaceous animals — herbivorous dinosaurs, crocodiles, turtles, fish — all carried a heavier carbon signature than their inland relatives, a pattern that points to marine food entering the food web at its base. First author Clayton Forster (University of Arkansas) argues the only candidate common to coastlines, absent inland, and available for millions of years is macroalgae: seaweed. The control is elegant — the inland-dwelling Tenontosaurus shows purely terrestrial signatures, so the readings are diet, not diagenesis. Published in Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution.

NASA's NICER neutron-star telescope ends science operations after 8 years

NASA announced November 17 that NICER, the Neutron star Interior Composition Explorer riding the International Space Station, has formally ended science operations after six and a half years beyond its planned mission. A failed internal motor froze the telescope's rotation in June 2025 and the instrument never recovered; teams now move to finalizing its 8-year X-ray archive and detaching it from its perch near the starboard solar arrays. NICER made the first precise radius measurements of neutron stars and demonstrated X-ray pulsar navigation — the dead-star census it leaves behind will keep producing results for years.

350 years ago this month, Ole Rømer proved light has a finite speed

In November 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer predicted — and it came true on November 9 — that an eclipse of Jupiter's moon Io would arrive ten minutes late, and then explained why: light takes time to cross Earth's orbit. Britannica confirms the sequence, including that his estimate of 22 minutes to traverse the orbital diameter was closer than anyone's for centuries, and that his own boss Cassini refused to accept it. A good anniversary read on how a prediction from moon-watch bookkeeping overturned the Cartesian idea of instantaneous light.

ferwen traces a line from the American Museum of Natural History's 1923 Gobi Desert expedition — the first reported dinosaur egg nests, one with a small beaked dinosaur slumped beside the clutch — straight into H.P.

An Empirical Study of Harness Design for Coding Agents

The authors hold the execution loop fixed while varying three harness components — planning, action space, and context management — measuring 176 matched settings across four models on SWE-Bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.1. It gives a rare component-level view of what actually moves agent performance, and shows how much of the gain comes from context management rather than the model. Score Centering Stabilizes Off-policy Reinforcement Learning

The authors show that RL instability under the training-inference mismatch is driven by a persistent drift that accumulates each step, and derive an additive 'score centering' correction that cancels it. Tested on models from 0.6B to 30B parameters, the correction matches or beats more complex fixes. For anyone doing RL for LLMs it is a cheap, well-motivated fix for one of the most common failure modes of off-policy training.

Venus may have swallowed its own moon

A UC Riverside study in The Astrophysical Journal argues Venus may once have had a moon that its extremely slow 243-day spin caused to spiral inward and crash into the planet. Simulations across moon masses from half to ten times the Moon's sent them crashing, with bigger moons falling faster. It is a genuinely surprising answer to why Earth's near-identical twin has no moon, and it opens the question of whether slow-rotating exoplanets can keep their moons.

Supermassive black hole winds are 100 times more powerful than scientists thought

XRISM observations of the quasar H1821+643 show winds from its supermassive black hole carry roughly 100 times more energy than earlier estimates, with turbulence spreading about 300,000 light-years — far beyond the host galaxy. The energy rivals several billion supernovae, per Tohoku University, and the work is in Nature Astronomy. It shows how much of a role these black holes actually play in moving gas and energy through their clusters.

Scientists are about to test Einstein's gravity with exotic matter

Researchers at ETH Zurich and the Paul Scherrer Institute built a high-intensity beam of 'cold' muonium — an exotic atom of an antimuon and an electron — using superfluid helium as an atomic cannon. The beam is finally suited for a gravity interferometer that could test the equivalence principle on a second-generation particle for the first time. If muonium falls differently from ordinary matter, it would point to new physics, including a hypothetical fifth force.
